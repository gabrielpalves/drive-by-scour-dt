\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

\subsection{Motivation}
\label{sec:motivation}

Scour --- the removal of riverbed material from around bridge foundations by
flowing water --- is consistently identified as a leading cause of bridge
failure~\cite{wardhana2003analysis, arneson2012hec18}. For railway networks the
exposure is systemic: a single scour-critical crossing can close a line, and
probabilistic assessments of the British railway network attribute a
substantial share of its weather-related risk to bridge scour during
floods~\cite{lamb2019scour}. Scour develops fastest during high flows, exactly
when visual and diver inspections are most dangerous and least reliable, and
scour holes can refill with loose sediment before an inspection takes
place~\cite{prendergast2014review, arneson2012hec18}. Bridges also suffer other
forms of deterioration, such as cracking and the loss of free rotation at
bearings, which may coexist with scour. Instrumenting every vulnerable bridge
permanently is rarely economical for a large inventory, which motivates
indirect approaches that reuse the vehicles already crossing the structures.

Drive-by monitoring extracts information about a bridge from the dynamic
response of a traversing vehicle, an idea introduced for bridge-frequency
identification by Yang et al.~\cite{yang2004extracting} and since developed
into a broad research field~\cite{malekjafarian2015review}, including dedicated
railway applications~\cite{braganca2023review, souza2023review}. The physical
basis for drive-by scour monitoring is established: foundation-stiffness loss
changes the dynamics of the soil--foundation--structure
system~\cite{prendergast2013investigation, prendergast2016determining}.
Wavelet-based analyses of simulated train-borne signals identified localized
scour signatures~\cite{fitzgerald2019drive}; scour-induced stiffness loss has
been identified from field-measured drive-by data~\cite{tola2025scour}; and
McGeown et al.~\cite{mcgeown2024pitch} showed, numerically and in a laboratory
model, that the pitch of a crossing carriage changes when a pier loses
stiffness. Machine-learning pipelines have classified scour severity and
multiple damage types under operational
variability~\cite{fernandes2024driveby, fernandes2025early,
fernandes2026early, fernandes2026canelas}.

\subsection{Drive-by damage classification in railway bridges}
\label{sec:related}

This study builds on the drive-by programme developed by Fernandes and
co-workers. Fernandes et al.~\cite{fernandes2024driveby} detected scour,
modeled as a local loss of pier-foundation stiffness, with an unsupervised deep
autoencoder operating on raw vertical accelerations. Sensors on the front and
rear bogies of the first and last vehicles detected 5\% and 10\% scour, with the
best results for the rear bogie of the last vehicle, whereas carbody sensors
were less effective. Fernandes et
al.~\cite{fernandes2025early} then classified ten scenarios combining cracks,
bearing damage, and scour from the vertical accelerations of the carbody and
front bogie of the leading vehicle. Their pipeline applies min--max
normalization and Piecewise Aggregate Approximation (PAA) to the signals and
classifies them with a CNN whose architecture and hyperparameters are tuned by
Bayesian optimization; accuracy is reported through the confusion matrix of the
best of 20 runs and boxplots of the per-scenario accuracy. Fernandes et
al.~\cite{fernandes2026early} classified scour levels with a supervised CNN and
showed the value of adding the train speed as an input, and Fernandes et
al.~\cite{fernandes2026canelas} extended the approach to a finite-element model
of the Canelas railway bridge calibrated to field modal parameters, using a
Bayesian-optimized CNN--LSTM.

Three wider strands are also relevant. First, drive-by learning studies often
keep the rail-irregularity profile fixed within a scenario while varying speed,
mass, and noise~\cite{locke2020using, fernandes2025early}, so the profile
handling must be stated as an experimental choice. Second, sprung-mass
responses are filtered by the suspension, whereas unsprung or axle-level
responses act nearer the wheel--rail interface~\cite{molodova2011axle,
malekjafarian2015review}, which motivates comparing responses at different
suspension levels. Third, residual concepts based on two sensors can cancel the
shared profile content~\cite{keenahan2014use, obrien2015apparent}, so a pair of
responses may contain information that neither member provides alone.

\subsection{Research gap and objectives}
\label{sec:gap}

These studies establish feasibility, but three aspects of the reported evidence
limit its interpretation. First, the accuracy of a supervised drive-by
classifier is often summarized by the confusion matrix of its best training
run, although networks trained from different random initializations may
perform very differently and some runs may fail to learn at all. Second, each study tunes its networks for
one or two preselected sensor positions, so other vehicle responses have not
been compared under a common protocol. Rotational responses are a notable gap:
Fernandes et al.~\cite{fernandes2025early} listed the integration of rotational
measurements as future work, and McGeown et al.~\cite{mcgeown2024pitch} found
carriage pitch to be sensitive to scour. Third, to the authors' knowledge, no
published drive-by classification benchmark has been independently reproduced,
so it is unclear how much of a reported accuracy depends on implementation
choices that the publications do not describe.

This paper addresses these gaps by reconstructing the ten-class multi-damage
benchmark of Fernandes et al.~\cite{fernandes2025early} and extending it to six
responses of the leading vehicle and to a CNN with multi-rate pooling. Every
configuration is trained with 20 random initializations on the same data
partition, and the complete distributions are reported. The study addresses
four questions:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Q1.} Can the published accuracies be reproduced with the
  published protocol, and how much do they vary between training runs?
  \item \textbf{Q2.} Which response of the leading vehicle carries the most
  information about the damage scenario?
  \item \textbf{Q3.} Does replacing the flatten layer of the CNN with
  multi-rate pooling improve the classification?
  \item \textbf{Q4.} Does a second response improve on the best single
  response?
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Contributions}
\label{sec:contributions}

The main contributions are as follows:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item A publication-faithful reconstruction of the benchmark that documents
  every deviation from the source, reports the 20-run distribution of each
  configuration, and shows that the published best-run accuracies are not
  recovered, not even by the published finalist architectures trained with
  Keras defaults, and that the validation score used for tuning overestimates
  the test accuracy.
  \item A comparison of six vehicle responses under shared and channel-specific
  hyperparameters, which identifies the carbody pitch rate as the most
  informative response and the bogie pitch rates as uninformative, together
  with a physical interpretation based on the vehicle geometry.
  \item An evaluation of multi-rate pooling, which yields the most stable
  classifier with a small fraction of the parameters of the flatten-based CNNs.
  \item A damage-type analysis showing that, in this benchmark, the crack
  label rather than scour or bearing damage limits the classification
  accuracy.
\end{enumerate}
